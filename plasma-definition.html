Plasma: what happens when a gas gets too hot to hold its electrons

Plasma is the fourth state of matter. Keep adding heat to a gas and its atoms collide so hard that electrons are knocked off. What's left is a glowing soup of free electrons and positive ions. Drag the heat slider or tap a state to watch it happen.

Waste-to-energy: plasma gasification using steam

A research project I worked on at the Technical University of Denmark. A plasma torch turns steam into a jet of plasma that is thousands of degrees hot. Waste dropped into that heat doesn't burn. It breaks down into syngas, a mixture of hydrogen and carbon monoxide that can make electricity or fuels. What's left melts into a harmless glassy slag. Tap a step, or play the tour.

Why use steam as the plasma-forming gas?

More hydrogen

The water itself becomes fuel. Hydrogen from the steam ends up as H₂, so the syngas is richer in hydrogen and has more energy.

No nitrogen

Air is about 78% nitrogen, which dilutes the syngas and lowers its heating value. Steam brings only hydrogen and oxygen.

Gentle oxidiser

Oxygen from the steam turns carbon into CO rather than burning it to CO₂, and its radicals (O, OH) help crack tars.

The catch

Steam plasma is strongly oxidising and its arc is harder to keep stable, so the electrodes erode and oxidise faster. That is the engineering problem to solve.
